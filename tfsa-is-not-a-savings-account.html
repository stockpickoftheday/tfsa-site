A TFSA Is Not a Savings Account

The name is the problem. “Tax-Free Savings Account” sounds like a savings account with no tax. It is not. It is an account that can hold almost any investment — and most Canadians who treat it like a savings account are leaving money on the table.

What most Canadians get wrong

About 65% of Canadians hold a TFSA, but 39% of holders use it just to hold cash, not to invest. Among Gen Z and Millennials, 41% of holders keep their TFSA in cash. That comes from a TD survey of 1,500 Canadian adults in October 2025. Cash in a TFSA earns a little interest, tax-free — but it is the lowest-return thing you can put in one.

The same survey found that 74% of Gen Z Canadians without a TFSA said lack of knowledge was the biggest barrier. The account is not the problem. Knowing what it can do is.

What a TFSA really is

A TFSA is a registered account — a tax shelter with a wrapper around it. What goes inside the wrapper is up to you. It can hold:

  • Cash and GICs — safe, but the return is small.
  • Stocks — shares of companies listed on the TSX, NYSE or NASDAQ.
  • ETFs and mutual funds — a basket of stocks or bonds in one holding.
  • Bonds — government and corporate bonds.

The tax-free growth is the same no matter what you hold. The difference is what the growth is. A dollar in cash might earn a few cents a year. A dollar in a broad stock-market ETF has historically earned far more over a decade — with more ups and downs along the way.

Why the mix-up costs money

Take two people, each with $38,566 in a TFSA — the average amount per holder in 2024, according to the Canada Revenue Agency. One keeps it in cash earning 2% a year. The other holds a broad stock-market ETF earning 7% a year on average. After 20 years, the cash holder has about $57,000. The ETF holder has about $149,000. Same account, same tax-free treatment — very different result. The gap is not the account. It is what is inside it.

These are rough numbers to show the point, not a promise. Markets go up and down, and past returns are not a guarantee.

What to do about it

  • Check what is in your TFSA today. If it is all cash, ask yourself what the money is for.
  • Money you need soon — a down payment next year, an emergency fund — belongs in cash or a GIC.
  • Money you will not need for years can work harder in stocks or ETFs, where the tax-free growth matters most.
  • You do not need a lot of money to start. Many brokerages let you buy one ETF share at a time.

Common questions

Is a TFSA a savings account?

No. A TFSA is a registered account that can hold cash, GICs, stocks, ETFs, bonds and mutual funds. A savings account at a bank is just one thing you can hold inside it — and usually the lowest-return one.

Why do so many Canadians keep cash in their TFSA?

Mostly lack of knowledge: a TD survey in October 2025 found 39% of TFSA holders keep their money in cash, and 74% of Gen Z Canadians without a TFSA said lack of knowledge was the biggest barrier.

Should I invest my TFSA or keep it in cash?

It depends on when you need the money. Money you need soon belongs in cash or a GIC. Money you will not need for years can work harder in stocks or ETFs, where tax-free growth matters most. This is general information, not financial advice.
